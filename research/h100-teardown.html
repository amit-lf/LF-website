The H100's bill of materials is public. Its bill of contracts is not — but its shape is. Here's the chip that defined the AI boom, disassembled into the agreements that govern each layer, and the clauses where the exposure actually sits.

Strip the package down and every physical layer answers to a different agreement — a different counterparty, a different failure mode. Four layers, four contracts, one chip. And the same four-contract stack governs Blackwell and GB200 too — only tighter.

What a teardown can — and can't — see

You can't just pull the actual contract.

A physical teardown X-rays the package. The contractual one hits a harder wall: the real NVIDIA–TSMC and NVIDIA–SK Hynix agreements are crown jewels — unfiled, or redacted past the point of use. Nobody outside the parties reads the specific deal.

But the shape is knowable. Dozens of comparable wafer-supply and IP-licensing agreements sit unredacted in SEC filings — read enough and you learn what a contract of that kind typically stipulates, and where the risk concentrates. The memory and packaging chokepoints show up another way: in public capacity disclosure.

The tightest link

The chokepoint isn't the die. It's memory and packaging.

The logic die is hard to make — but the binding constraints sit in the two layers around it: HBM memory and CoWoS packaging. Both run on allocation, prepay, and qualification lock-in, and both are booked out well ahead of demand. A contract problem in either layer strands an otherwise-finished chip.

One chip. A stack of contracts.

Every H100 is silicon — and underneath the silicon sits a foundry agreement, a packaging agreement, a memory supply agreement, and a web of IP licenses, each with its own counterparty and its own way to fail. The bill of materials is only half the story. The bill of contracts is the other half.
