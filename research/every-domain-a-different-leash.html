Every Domain, a Different Leash

How the terms shift across AI compute, analog, memory, optics — and the foundries themselves

Every chip company signs a version of the same few deals — a foundryA factory that manufactures chips designed by others (e.g. TSMC, GlobalFoundries). to make its parts, a customer to buy them, a licensor for the IP underneath. We read the filed agreements behind nine recent chip IPOs. Read one at a time, they're nine stories. Read them by domain, and a sharper pattern shows up: the kind of chip you build predicts the kind of capture you sign.

Different leash every time — but always a leash, and always readable in the exhibit.

Build AI compute or networking — your leash is your customer

When your buyer is also your investor and your lender, the paper stops being a supply agreement and becomes a claim. Cerebras filed eleven supply agreements — every one with G42 or its orbit. A $300M prepayment came with a catch: "any of Cerebras's rights (including ownership rights) in respect of the HPC infrastructure [***] will transfer to G42 US automatically" if the order slips. Aquantia sat in the mirror seat, selling to Intel: "Intel may terminate this Project Statement for convenience at any time … Aquantia may not terminate." One party holds the exit, the volume, and — via embedded mask works — the IP. In this domain, the counterparty across the table is the one writing your revenue.

Build analog, sensors or automotive silicon — your leash is your parent

These companies trade as independent and run through a controlling shareholder who owns the foundry, the IP, and a change-of-controlA change in who ultimately owns a company (e.g. a parent dropping below a threshold), which can trigger contract rights. tripwire. Allegro's royalty rights "shall terminate in the event that Sanken ceases to own … more than fifty percent." That clause sat in the exhibit for years; in 2024 the parent crossed the line and Allegro drew a ~$6.9B bid. Mobileye disclosed Intel's right "to maintain 80.1% ownership," and "Intel solely owns all Project IPR to Intel Core Technology." The parent isn't on the cap table — it's on every axis of the contract.

Build MEMS or memory — your leash is your foundry, because your foundry owns your IP

Here the fab and the patent holder are the same company, and you can't leave. SiTime single-sources MEMS wafers from Bosch, which also licenses it the underlying MEMS technology — and the agreement unwinds only "upon providing three (3) years advance written notice," with Bosch's liability capped at $5M. Everspin invented MRAM and then agreed it "shall not disclose, dispose of or license any Foreground IP to any foundry during the Exclusivity Period" — no second source, by contract, on the line it funded and can be terminated from for a missed payment. The party that makes your chips also holds the technology that makes them possible.

Build optics — your leash is your licensor, because you don't own your "proprietary" technology at all

MetaOptics filed five agreements; all five are inbound licenses from one Singapore government agency, each "non-exclusive, non-sublicensable, non-transferable, revocable for cause." The agency took a ~5–6% equity stake as the license fee, collects royalties, audits the books, can license the same metalens IP to a rival, and disclaims any warranty that the technology is "free from infringement." "Proprietary" turns out to be a rented word.

And the foundries everyone else leans on? They're captive upstream

The supplier at the bottom of a dozen supply chains has its own single point of failure. GlobalFoundries buys its SOI substrate from one French supplier on a "Rolling Frozen Delivery Plan" with a "minimum of [***] per calendar year," and is itself a "controlled company" of an Abu Dhabi sovereign fund. SkyWater — America's "trusted" foundry — licenses its core process from its former parent and hands its own improvements back: "Licensee hereby irrevocably transfers, conveys, and assigns to Licensor … all … Licensor Improvements." Concentration doesn't stop at the fab. It just gets harder to see.

Why it's like this

None of this is a negotiating failure — it's structural. A specialty-chip company usually can't build its own fab (the capability is scarce and capital-intensive), often didn't originate its core IP (it's inherited from a parent or licensed from a lab), and needs an anchor customer or parent to get funded in the first place. At the moment of signing, the counterparty is the only one who has what the company needs — the process, the IP, the capital, or the demand. So the leash gets signed. It's priced in at birth, and it's why the shape is so predictable by domain.

The point — and what you can do about it

The leash changes shape — customer, parent, foundry, licensor, upstream — but it's always there, and it's readable. You don't have to read a contract cold to know where to look: AI compute, check whether your customer is also your investor; analog or automotive, find the change-of-control tripwire and the parent's IP; MEMS or memory, ask whether your foundry also owns your process and how long the exit takes; optics, ask what you actually own versus license; and whoever your foundry is, ask who owns them.

You can't always escape the leash — a MEMS startup still needs its foundry. But two of the three forces behind it, information and experience, are fixable. The counterparty's whole edge is that it has signed its version a hundred times and you've signed once. Reading a hundred real deals hands you that experience — so you sign knowingly, and you find the buried liability first, on your timeline, not when an acquirer finds it at your next round.

Most companies sign once. Knowing your domain's characteristic leash is how you read the exhibit before someone else reads it for you.
