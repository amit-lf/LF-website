Chapter 1

In late 2025, carmakers began warning of production stoppages. The cause was not a fab fire or a demand spike. It was a dispute over the ownership of a company called Nexperia — a maker of diodes and transistors, among the cheapest components on any circuit board, and among the last a buyer would think to worry about. A single plant going quiet was enough to put assembly lines at risk within about a month.

The instructive part of the story is not the event. It is that the exposure behind it had been sitting in public view — dated and documented — for years.

Two clocks

It helps to separate two things that move at very different speeds.

The event — the export dispute, the plant stoppage, the parts not arriving — took roughly a month from the first headline to the shortage. Nobody forecasts a thing like that; it turns on a ministerial decision in a capital far from the assembly line. On this clock, prediction is hopeless.

The exposure — being single-sourcedBuying a part from only one supplier, with no qualified alternative. on a supplier whose ultimate owner you could not see, under contracts that carried no obligation to disclose that owner and no right to act if it changed — had been in place for years. On this clock, nothing was hidden.

Nobody forecasts the lightning. Anybody can notice they have been standing in dry brush.

The exposure, dated and public

Every link in the chain was a matter of public record, and each carried a date:

  • 2019 — Nexperia, a long-established European chipmaker, came under the control of Wingtech, a company based in China. The parent had no machine-readable ownership record in the standard entity registries — so an automated screen of "Nexperia" returned the subsidiary and stopped there.
  • December 2024 — the United States added Wingtech, the parent, to its Entity List. Nexperia itself still screened clean.
  • September 2025 — a US rule extended those restrictions to reach majority-owned subsidiaries of listed parents — reaching through the clean-looking name to the company underneath.
  • Late 2025 — Dutch authorities took control of Nexperia under an emergency law; China then restricted exports from Nexperia's main finishing plant in Dongguan, which completes the majority of the company's output. With that plant restricted, the parts stopped, and carmakers faced line-down risk.
  • November 2025 — the two governments stepped back; the flow resumed and lines restarted.

    • There was an even earlier signal. Three years before the stoppage — in November 2022 — the United Kingdom had ordered Nexperia to divest its Newport wafer plant, on ownership-based national-security grounds. A government had already acted, publicly, on this exact exposure, in a different jurisdiction, years ahead.

    The geometry: one plant, one unseen owner

    Two ordinary features of a supply relationship combined into a single point of failure.

    The first was concentration. The majority of Nexperia's parts were finished at one plant. Qualifying an alternative source for an automotive-grade component takes months, so single-sourcing is common — and here it meant one facility, in one country, sat astride the supply.

    The second was ownership you could not see. The party on the contract was a clean European name; the party that ultimately controlled it was not visible to an automated screen. A publicly filed Nexperia agreement says nothing about who owns the company — no obligation to disclose the ultimate owner, and no right to act if control changes. The silence is the finding.

    Put together: a cheap, single-sourced part, made in one plant, controlled by an owner the buyer's systems could not resolve, under a contract that neither disclosed that owner nor gave the buyer a way out.

    The event ended. The exposure did not.

    It is tempting to file the episode as history — the parts flowed again, the lines restarted. But November 2025 was a diplomatic pause, not a structural repair. As of this writing the ownership is unchanged, the parent remains on the Entity List, the automated screen still cannot resolve the parent, the finishing concentration still sits at one plant, and the contracts that lacked disclosure and change-of-controlA change in who ultimately owns a company (e.g. an acquisition), which can trigger contract rights. rights still lack them. The exposure is not something that happened. It is something that is still open.

    What can — and cannot — be read

    None of this is a prediction. The point is the opposite: because the event was unforecastable, the only defensible work is on the exposure — and the exposure was documentary the whole time. What a buyer can read, on any supplier, from public records and its own contracts:

  • Who ultimately owns the counterparty — and, just as important, where that ownership cannot be resolved. A screen that says "we cannot see the owner here — look closer" is worth more than a false all-clear.
  • Where the supply is single-threaded — one plant, one region, no qualified second sourceA qualified alternative supplier for the same part..
  • What the contract does not say — no disclosure obligation, no change-of-control right, no allocation priority in a shortage.

    • None of these requires forecasting anything. They require reading what is already written down.

    Why this is a product, not an anecdote

    The obvious objection is that anyone could have looked up Nexperia's owner in five minutes. True — for one supplier. A carmaker or a Tier-1 has thousands. The manual version is not hard; it is impossible by volume. The value is not in researching every supplier — it is in knowing which forty of your two thousand suppliers you cannot resolve an owner for, so a person can spend five minutes each on the forty that matter. That is a shorter list, not more information.

    The paper trail is not the limitation. It is the whole opportunity: the exposures that are written down are precisely the ones nobody reads.

    Chapter 2

    The general field guide covers the twelve dimensions of risk on any semiconductor contract — take-or-payA promise to pay for a set volume whether or not you actually order it., single-sourceBuying a part from only one supplier, with no qualified alternative. supply, one-sided liability caps, the change-of-controlA change in who ultimately owns a company (e.g. an acquisition), which can trigger contract rights. exposure nobody signs. All of them apply when the chip goes in a car. But automotive adds its own grammar, and it is the part that surprises a buyer who treats a chip contract like any other purchase agreement.

    Three things make automotive different: the parts are safety-critical, the programs run for fifteen years or more, and every part must pass a qualificationThe formal, months-long process of validating that a supplier or fab makes a part to your spec before you can use it in production. cycle measured in months before it can ship. Together they turn ordinary contract terms into multi-year locks — and they create a handful of clauses that generic contract review, and even a general semiconductor read, will miss. This is a companion to the field guide, on what changes in a car.

    Qualification lock-in — AEC-Q100, IATF 16949, PPAP

    Before an automotive chip goes into production it clears three gates: AEC-Q100The automotive reliability standard a chip must pass to be used in vehicles. (the device passes automotive reliability stress testing), IATF 16949 (the supplier's site runs an automotive quality system), and PPAPProduction Part Approval Process — the auto-industry sign-off that a part is production-ready. (a Production Part Approval Process sign-off that the specific part, from that specific line, is production-ready). That process takes months and real money.

    The idiosyncrasy. Because qualification is expensive and slow, a qualified part is a locked-in part — switching sources means re-running the gates. Worse, the supplier can force that on you: a fab move, a process change, or a site transfer can trigger re-qualification and a fresh PPAP. The contract decides who bears that cost and that time.

    Who carries it. Usually the buyer — the default is that a supplier can change its process and the burden of re-qualifying lands downstream. Advice: pin down, in writing, who pays for and schedules re-qualification on a supplier-initiated process or fab change. A clause barring unilateral process changes without re-qualification at the supplier's cost is worth far more than it reads.

    PCN flow-down — and the notice gap

    A Product Change Notification (PCNProduct Change Notification — advance notice a supplier owes you before changing or discontinuing a part.) is the advance warning a supplier owes before it changes or discontinues a part. In consumer electronics it is a courtesy. In automotive it is load-bearing, because of a gap most buyers never measure.

    The idiosyncrasy. The PCN notice window in your supply contract is often shorter than the re-qualification timeline the change forces on you — an AEC-Q100 re-qual runs roughly six to eighteen months depending on the device. So you can receive perfectly compliant notice and still be caught short, with no qualified part when the change lands.

    Who carries it. The buyer, sitting in the gap between the notice window and the re-qual clock. Advice: measure the two against each other. If your PCN notice is shorter than your device's re-qualification time, that difference is your exposure — negotiate the window up, or secure a bridge-supply obligation to cover it.

    Longevity, EOL, and the last-time-buy

    Cars are built for years and serviced for a decade or more after that. A chip's commercial life is shorter — the supplier would rather move you to the next part.

    The idiosyncrasy. The end-of-life (EOL) and last-time-buy (LTB) terms decide whether you can secure supply across your whole program and service tail, and on what notice. Without them, a supplier can discontinue a part on its schedule, not yours, and leave you unable to build or repair a platform still on the road.

    Who carries it. The buyer left holding an un-serviceable program. Advice: get a longevity commitment matched to your program life, plus a last-time-buy window long enough to bridge to a redesign — and confirm the LTB quantity math actually covers production and the service tail, not just the next model year.

    The OEM directed-buy squeeze

    Since the 2021–22 shortage that idled auto plants worldwide, carmakers have gone direct to chipmakers — negotiating supply and, increasingly, directing the Tier-1 on whom to buy from (GM with Wolfspeed and Qualcomm, Ford with GlobalFoundries, among others).

    The idiosyncrasy. The Tier-1 keeps the purchasing obligation and the risk but loses the leverage — it is committed to a source it did not choose and cannot freely renegotiate, while the OEM holds the relationship above it. It becomes the meat in the sandwich: bound to the supplier's terms below and the OEM's direction above.

    Who carries it. The Tier-1, squeezed on both sides. Advice: separate what is OE-directed from what you actually control — your negotiating leverage lives in the non-directed parts. Where a part is directed, push to flow the OEM's commitments back-to-back onto the supplier, so you are not carrying a take-or-pay to a source the OEM chose without the OEM standing behind the volume.

    Where the general dimensions bite harder

    The field guide's twelve still apply — they just cut deeper in a car:

  • Take-or-pay — which Tier-1s mostly avoid, precisely because the OEM won't guarantee demand; where you're forced into one (single-source, or an OEM-backed program), you're committed to volume the vehicle line may never draw.
  • Single-source on a safety-critical part with no qualified alternative — the exact geometry that idled plants in 2021, and the reason the Nexperia reading matters.
  • Change of controlA change in who ultimately owns a company (e.g. an acquisition), which can trigger contract rights. on a supplier whose ultimate owner you cannot see — a cheap part, one plant, an owner your screens miss.
  • Force majeureA clause excusing a party from performing when something beyond its control (disaster, war, a government act) stops it. and allocation in a shortage: you are one of many customers, and the allocation rule is written by the party who is short.

    • The through-line

    The general field guide tells you who carries the risk on any chip contract. Automotive multiplies that risk three ways — the qualification lock that makes every part hard to leave, the service-life horizon that outlasts the part, and the OEM above you that holds the leverage while you hold the obligation. Read those before you sign a multi-year program, not after a line goes down.
