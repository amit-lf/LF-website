If you read one page

Three things your suppliers have disclosed that are worth acting on

1

The increases are price, not volume — and the makers publish both numbers

SK Hynix reported DRAM bit shipments flat quarter on quarter while average selling price rose mid‑60%, with a record 72% operating margin in the same quarter. Samsung reported blended DRAM ASP up low 90%. Kioxia put price at 70% of the revenue driver with volume up low single digits. Micron: revenue +74% on mid‑single‑digit bit growth.

Why it matters: with volume flat, there is no fixed‑cost absorption to explain the margin, and no volume growth for a cost argument to rest on. Micron is the one maker that has said its own bit costs are rising — credit that, and note its margin still went from 44.7% to 84.6% in four quarters. Section 03.

2

Whether to prepay for allocation is arithmetic, and the number is about 3%

NetApp earned $113m of interest income on roughly $3.6bn of cash and short‑term investments — about 3% pre‑tax. Samsung states advance payments are “a contractual requirement”; SK Hynix incorporates deposits. A prepayment is worth making only if the discount it secures beats about 3% a year over the period the cash is held, before any allowance for volume you may not need.

It converts a strategy debate into a threshold a supplier can be asked to beat. It also competes directly with the $1.0bn buyback authorized in May 2026, which makes it a capital decision rather than a procurement one. Section 06, conflict 2.

3

Two of the five have said their own prices are destroying demand

SK Hynix: “demand softening have emerged… due to cost pressures stemming from higher memory prices” in PC and mobile. Kioxia, independently: the PC market will see “a decrease in shipments due to rising BOM costs, leading to a slight decline in NAND demand”.

These are quotable admissions from a named quarter, and they mean the tight‑supply account is not uniform across end markets — by the suppliers' own statements. Sections 05 and 06.

What we are not telling you: no price forecast, no supplier score, and no claim that a particular increase is unjustified — blended ASP across a product line is not the price of your part. The one thing worth sending us is a price increase letter; it is a single document and the answer is checkable against the quarter it cites.

01

What this is

This is a picture of the five companies NetApp buys memory and drives from. Each files public accounts and holds public earnings calls, and this report reads them from the buyer's side of the table. The analysis is on the latest filed year and the one before it — 2025 for four of the five, 2026 for Seagate. Earlier years appear only where a single reference point is needed, and section 04 says where and why.

It is not a report about how NetApp is performing — that is already in NetApp's own filing and NetApp already knows it. What its filing does carry is NetApp's stated strategy and capital position, and section 02 quotes those so that section 06 can test the supplier picture against what NetApp has said it wants rather than against anything we assumed.

The stock in this chain sits upstream and the commitments sit downstream. That is a defensible way to run a balance sheet, and it also means the five companies in section 05 are the ones whose position decides what a renewal costs.

What this report will not do

  • It does not forecast a price. Nothing here predicts DRAM, NAND or drive pricing for a future quarter. Section 06 is about 2027, and every row in it is a dated statement a named supplier made about its own plans — their calendar, not our projection.
  • It gives no single score. There is no blended supplier rating, because a blended number hides which dimension moved and can come out flattering for every supplier at once. Each dimension is reported on its own.
  • It does not split any of these five into fixed and variable cost. Our cost model is deliberately withheld for memory, and the reason is set out in section 08.
  • Where the filings will not carry an answer, it says so rather than estimating one.

A note on what absence means. Where a figure is not shown for a supplier, that means it is not in the material we have read — not that the supplier does not report it. We never treat a gap in our own reading as a gap in a supplier's disclosure.

03

Price or volume: what the makers disclosed themselves

Across the four memory makers' most recent reported quarters, essentially all of the revenue increase came from price. Bit shipments were flat to low single digits.

This is not our calculation. Each maker discloses its own average selling price change and its own bit shipment change every quarter, and each has reported the same shape. Set beside their gross margins over the same quarters, it settles the question a cost‑justification letter asks you to treat as open.

Samsung attributes the increase to market pricing and product mix. Mix is a legitimate reason for a blended average to move, and it is not a cost increase either.

“The selling price went up by 70%, like for like blended ASP both increased by 70%. The quarter‑on‑quarter shipment volume also increased the low single digit.” — and on the next quarter, “ASP accounts for 70% as a driver.”

Yoshihiko Kawamura, CFO, Kioxia, Q1‑26 earnings call

Kioxia has quantified the split itself: price is 70% of the revenue driver, volume is low single digits. Its gross margin in that quarter was 78.1%, against 43.3% for the full prior year.

DRAM FQ2‑26: “Revenue increased 74% Q/Q · Bit shipments up mid‑single digits Q/Q · ASPs increased in mid‑60s percentage range”.

Micron FQ2‑26 investor presentation, Performance by technology

Same shape at Micron. Revenue up 74%, bits up mid‑single digits. Its filed gross margin moved from 44.7% in Q4‑25 to 84.6% in Q3‑26 across four quarters.

“Both of these trends to higher performance — as well as greenfield investments that take time to ramp scale and are not as efficient — both of these are going to be trends that we expect will actually increase DRAM bit costs here in the near term.”

Micron Q3‑26 earnings call

The counterweight, and it belongs here. Micron is the one maker that has said on the record that its own unit costs are rising, because of performance mix and the inefficiency of ramping new plants. That is a real cost argument and it should be credited. It also has to be read against the line above it: in the quarter Micron's ASP rose by the mid‑60s percent, its bits rose by mid‑single digits. A rising bit cost does not account for a margin that went from 44.7% to 84.6%.

What this does and does not prove

It does settle the price‑versus‑volume question, because the makers disclose both numbers and the answer is not close. When bits are flat and price is up by two thirds, revenue growth is price. That also removes the one objection that otherwise defeats a margin‑based argument — better fixed‑cost absorption at higher volume — because the volume is not there.

It does not prove any particular increase is unjustified. Micron has stated its bit costs are rising. A supplier may have higher input costs on a specific part, a mix shift, or a contract basis that differs from its blended average. Blended ASP across a whole product line is not the price of your part, and that gap is exactly what the ask in section 06 is for.

On reading the quarters as a direction of travel: each figure here is a disclosed quarterly number with its quarter named, and the attribution of cause comes from the maker's own sentence in that quarter rather than from anything we fitted. We are not projecting the next one.

04

What the five have in common, and where they part

As of their latest filed year, all five have moved supply onto binding, multi‑year, part‑prepaid allocation, and three of the five are earning the best gross margin in their own record. But only one of the five has a capital programme behind it.

That last clause is the whole report. The five look alike from the outside and their cost cases are not alike at all, and the difference is visible in filed figures they published themselves.

  • What is not offered, and what is. Why filed accounts cannot settle whether a price increase is justified

It is tempting to read heavy capital spending as a legitimate cost case and light spending as margin recovery. That inference does not hold, and this report does not make it.

  • Capital spending is not cost. It never touches cost of goods sold; depreciation does, and it follows the spending by years. Micron's $15.9bn in fiscal 2025 raises its depreciation across the rest of the decade, not its cost this year — and Kioxia's 12% today sits alongside depreciation from the 40% it spent in 2022, which is still running through its accounts.
  • Light spending can mean the plant is already built. The same low ratio supports the opposite conclusion depending on where a supplier is in its own build cycle.
  • Memory and drive prices are set by supply and demand, not by cost. Micron's gross margin moved from −9.1% to 45.2% without its cost base moving anything like that far. For a commodity, “the market cleared here” is an honest answer rather than an evasion, and a buyer who insists on a cost justification may get a worse answer than one who negotiates on leverage.
  • Capital intensity tracks technology node and product mix. NAND needs less capital per bit than leading‑edge DRAM or HBM, so the ratios partly measure what a supplier makes rather than how it prices.
  • Rising gross margin would not prove price outran cost on its own. It also results from better fixed‑cost absorption at higher volume, which Micron's own commentary calls the primary lever on its margin. Separating the two needs bit shipments and average selling price, which are not in the filed annual accounts — but the makers disclose both every quarter on their calls, and section 03 uses what they have said. That is why the price‑versus‑volume question is settled in this report and the cost‑justification question is not.
  • Three of the five report in won or yen. An increase quoted in dollars can be a flat price in a supplier's own currency.

What the filed accounts do establish, and what this report rests on instead: none of the five is loss‑making, so any argument that an increase is needed for viability is contradicted by their own statements. Everything beyond that comes from what management has said about its own pricing — Seagate that it prices on value rather than cost, SK Hynix that customers are “prioritizing procurement over price” — which are admissions, not inferences of ours. The cost question itself is testable, and the test needs unit cost data only the supplier holds. Section 06 asks for it rather than scoring them on capital spending.

The obligation runs both ways. A volume commitment a buyer cannot hit becomes the buyer's exposure.

“With all of the efforts that we are doing, we still don't have line of sight as to when the supply is going to be able to meet demand, because demand continues to escalate at a very rapid pace over time.”

Micron Q3‑26 earnings call

Where the argument is

Not on capital spending, which is not cost. The argument is which product the increase is priced for. Micron has tied its programme to HBM and leading‑edge DRAM; separate that from the parts actually being bought, and ask for unit cost on the line being quoted — cost per bit or per module, for the specific part, in the quarter cited. That is the only form of the cost question that can be answered.

“As this supply demand imbalance persists, customers are prioritizing procurement over price… Therefore, the favorable pricing environment is expected to continue for the time being.”

SK Hynix Q1‑26

“Customers are prioritizing procurement over price” is a statement about buyer behavior, not about cost. Read next to the first quote in this profile, it describes a supplier that knows its prices are too high somewhere and expects to hold them anyway.

Where the argument is

SK Hynix has the highest margins of the five and mid‑range reinvestment, so a cost‑driven increase is the hardest to sustain here. Its own two statements sit against each other: prices are softening demand in PC and mobile, and the pricing environment is expected to stay favorable. Ask which of the two applies to enterprise, and put the Yongin date in the contract.

Price the prepayment, and get the 2028 claim written as a condition rather than accepted as context.

1. Multi‑sourcing is still possible, but it is no longer free

NetApp

“We strive to have multiple suppliers qualified to provide critical components where possible… to mitigate our supply chain risk.”

Form 10‑K FY2026

Samsung Electronics

“we are negotiating the contractual terms, giving priority to customers willing to accept mutually binding contractual conditions and who can provide assurance about committed demand.”

Q2‑26 earnings call

Qualifying a second supplier used to cost engineering time. Now each qualified supplier also wants binding volume and, at Samsung and SK Hynix, cash. Two qualified sources means two sets of commitments covering demand that will only be filled once.

A viable path — our judgment

Keep the multi‑source objective but move it from two suppliers per component to one committed source plus one qualified and uncommitted source per technology. Carry the commitment where the price of commitment is lowest, and hold the uncommitted leg wherever a supplier still has open volume.

On the filed record, the uncommitted leg in NAND is Kioxia. It is targeting only 50% of shipments under long‑term agreement by 2028, where Samsung is already at 60–70% of capacity committed. That is an availability argument and it stands on its own; we are not resting it on Kioxia's capital spending, which section 04 explains cannot settle a pricing question either way.

Derived from: NetApp's stated multi‑source objective, plus Kioxia's own 50%‑by‑2028 target and Samsung's own 60–70% figure, plus the capital‑spending comparison in Kioxia's profile. What we do not know: whether Kioxia is qualified on the parts in question, or can be. That is an engineering answer, not a filings one.

Ask the committed supplierIf you are asking for binding volume and a deposit, you are asking us to stop dual‑sourcing this part. Price that. What does the single‑source version cost against the dual‑source version, on the same volume?

2. Whether to prepay is arithmetic, not a judgment call

NetApp

“This manufacturing strategy minimizes capital investments and overhead expenditures while creating flexibility for rapid expansion.” Interest income of $113m on about $3.6bn of cash and short‑term investments.

Form 10‑K FY2026

Samsung Electronics

“we've included substantial advance payments as a contractual requirement to be maintained over the multi‑year contract period. We've already received a significant portion of these payments…”

Q2‑26 earnings call

An advance payment held for the life of a multi‑year contract is capital tied up in a supplier's balance sheet. That is the opposite of the asset‑light model NetApp has described to its investors — and unlike a fab, it buys no asset.

A viable path — our judgment

Stop treating this as a strategic question and price it. NetApp earns about 3% pre‑tax on its cash. So a prepayment is worth making only if the discount it secures, or the price increase it avoids, exceeds roughly 3% a year over the period the cash is held — before any allowance for the risk that the committed volume is not needed.

That single test converts “should we prepay for allocation?” into a number a supplier can be asked to beat. Below the threshold, the asset‑light objective and the economics agree and the answer is no. Above it, prepaying is consistent with protecting gross margin, which section 02 shows NetApp names as an objective in its own right.

Derived from: two figures in NetApp's own filing — interest income of $113m and the $3.6bn cash and short‑term investments balance — set against Samsung's stated prepayment requirement. Where it is approximate: the yield uses the closing balance, not the average through the year, and a company may reasonably use its weighted cost of capital instead of its cash yield, which would raise the threshold rather than lower it.

AskPrice the same volume two ways — with the advance payment and without it. We will compare them on total cost including our own cost of that cash, and we will tell you the threshold the discount has to clear.

3. The commitment horizon is longer than the demand visibility

NetApp

“Our business is characterized by… short lead times and product delivery schedules. Accordingly, backlog may vary materially quarter to quarter and at any given time may not be a meaningful indicator of future revenue.”

Form 10‑K FY2026, Backlog

Seagate

“the vast majority of our nearline exabytes are now allocated into calendar 2028… many are actively seeking to extend planning horizons through 2029 and beyond”

Q4‑26 earnings call

This is the structural conflict and it is the one worth taking to a board. NetApp has told the market its own order book is not a meaningful indicator of future revenue, and its suppliers are asking it to commit quantities two and three years out. Nothing in NetApp's filing supports a demand forecast of that length.

A viable path — our judgment

Commit to capacity rather than to quantity. Pay to reserve a defined share of a supplier's output over the term, with the quantity drawn against it set quarterly inside a band. The supplier gets the planning visibility it says it is buying; NetApp does not sign a number it has told its own investors it cannot forecast.

The suppliers' own words suggest visibility, not volume, is what they are after. Micron says its agreements exist so that both sides “can count on” what is in them, and Samsung asks for “assurance about committed demand”. A reserved share with a quarterly draw provides that assurance. Whether any of the five will write it that way, we do not know — nothing in their public statements says they have.

Derived from: NetApp's own backlog disclosure set against Seagate's 2028 allocation horizon and Samsung's through‑2028 statement, plus Micron's and Samsung's descriptions of what their agreements are for. What we do not know: whether a share‑of‑output structure is on offer from any of them. Treat this as the position to open from, not a term we have seen agreed.

AskWe cannot forecast quantity three years out and our own filings say so. We can commit to a share of your output across the term. Will you price a reserved share with a quarterly draw inside an agreed band, instead of a fixed quantity?

4. Two suppliers have described the risk the $25m provision is covering

The provision is 2.5% of the non‑cancelable book, and it is set against NetApp's own demand forecast. A viable path — our judgment, and the limit of it

The useful question is not whether $25m is enough. It is at what level of demand decline the provision stops being enough, and then whether the non‑cancelable book should be shorter rather than better provided for.

We cannot answer that and will not estimate it. It needs NetApp's own demand forecast and the maturity profile of the $1.0bn, neither of which is public — the filing gives the total and the FY2027 split, not the detail. What the public record does establish is that the risk is not hypothetical, because two of the five suppliers have named it themselves.

Derived from: the $1.0bn and $25m figures as reported, plus the SK Hynix and Kioxia demand statements. What would answer it: the commitment maturity schedule and the demand forecast the provision is calculated against. That is an internal exercise, and it is the right one to run before the next commitment is signed rather than after.

Derived from: the fiscal 2026 capital returns and the May 2026 authorization as reported, plus SK Hynix's and Samsung's stated deposit and prepayment requirements. 6. NetApp is asked to manage component cost against suppliers who do not price on cost

NetApp

“our ability to maintain our gross margins, including managing component costs” — named as a forward‑looking objective in its own right.

Form 10‑K FY2026, forward‑looking statements

Seagate · SK Hynix

“Our value‑based pricing approach…” — “customers are prioritizing procurement over price… the favorable pricing environment is expected to continue for the time being.”

Seagate Q3‑26 · SK Hynix Q1‑26

NetApp has told the market it will manage component cost. Two of its five suppliers have told their own markets that cost is not what sets their price. A negotiation run as a cost audit against a supplier that prices on value and scarcity is arguing a case the other side has already declined to make.

A viable path — our judgment

Put the cost question once, in the only form that can be answered, and treat the answer as the fork. Ask for cost per bit or per module on the specific part in the quarter cited. A supplier that supplies it has made a cost claim that can be checked. A supplier that declines has told you it is a price decision, and the negotiation should move to leverage: volume, planning horizon, prepayment, and term.

Section 04 explains why we do not run this test from public accounts ourselves. Capital spending is not cost, gross margin expands on volume absorption as readily as on price, and separating the two requires bit shipments and average selling price that are not disclosed. The question is sound; only the supplier holds the data that answers it.

Derived from: NetApp's own stated objective on component cost, set against Seagate's and SK Hynix's own descriptions of how they price. What we do not know: whether any of the five will provide unit cost. None has offered it publicly, so treat a refusal as information rather than as a failed request.

AskIf this increase is a cost pass‑through, give us cost per bit or per module for this part in the quarter you have cited, and we will check it against your filed accounts. If you would rather not, then this is a price decision rather than a cost one — say so, and we will negotiate on what our volume and our planning horizon are worth to you.

Four of these six have a path we would act on. Two end in a question that needs data we do not have — NetApp's own demand forecast, and unit cost that only the supplier holds — and we have said so rather than filling the gap.

That is deliberate. A supplier picture built from public filings can tell you what to ask for and what a claim is worth. It cannot tell you what your own demand forecast will bear, and it cannot separate a cost increase from margin recovery without unit data nobody publishes. A report that pretends otherwise is worth less than one that marks the line.

What we are withholding, and why

Our cost model splits a supplier's cost base into a fixed part and a part that moves with revenue, and reports what a volume change does to it. We do not run it for any of these five, and that is a deliberate exclusion rather than a gap.

The reason is in the Micron table in section 05. For a memory or drive supplier, revenue is price multiplied by volume, and the two move independently — Micron's revenue went $30.8bn, then $15.5bn, then $37.4bn in three years, with gross margin between −9.1% and 45.2%. A revenue change cannot stand in for a volume change in that data, so a model built on revenue would return a fixed‑cost share that reads precisely and means nothing.

Their filed figures are a different matter and are used freely throughout this report. A fact and an inference get different treatment. A model that declines to answer where the data will not carry a number is worth more than one that fills the gap.

The same rule explains two other absences. There is no price forecast anywhere in this report, and no single score per supplier — a blended rating hides which dimension moved, and one we built came out flattering for every supplier at once because it was measuring how confidently management speaks rather than the business.
